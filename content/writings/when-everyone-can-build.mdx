When everyone can build, what really matters?

Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) explains something I’ve been feeling but couldn’t articulate — that the most meaningful engineering work isn’t just about writing elegant code, but about understanding the messy problems customers are actually trying to solve.

The part about being a “technical Swiss Army knife” hits home. The best projects I’ve worked on required jumping between writing code, debugging systems, explaining technical limitations to stakeholders, and prototyping completely different approaches when the original ask wasn’t the real problem.

Traditional engineering roles would split this across multiple teams — UX, Product, DevOps, Platform, etc — but owning that full journey is what makes the work rewarding.

What strikes me about the FDE model is how it legitimizes and normalizes something that feels natural — gravitating toward the complex projects that don’t fit neatly into standard product offerings. The ones that require understanding not just the tech stack but the customers business models.

The timing feels significant too. We’re at this point where AI capabilities are incredibly powerful but still need human judgment to deploy effectively. Customers come with problems they can’t even articulate properly — they know they want “AI magic” but don’t know what good looks like. That’s where the FDE mindset becomes valuable. You can’t just throw a model at their data and call it done.

As AI democratizes development tools, the value shifts to understanding the bigger picture — knowing which problems are worth solving, how solutions fit into existing systems, what the unintended consequences might be. When anyone can build basic integrations, the real differentiator becomes bridging the gap between what’s technically possible and what’s actually valuable.

The Forward Deployed Engineering (FDE) model isn’t just about being a better engineer or solution architect or even an engineering leader — it’s about being a better problem solver. Having the technical skills to understand what’s possible, the business sense to understand what’s valuable, and the judgment to understand what’s actually needed.

In a world where everyone can build anything, that combination becomes everything.
