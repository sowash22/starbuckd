When business leaders speak in results and engineers speak in deliverables, accountability gets lost in translation.

I came across an incredible piece by James Shore this week called “The Accountability Problem.” It perfectly captures something I’ve seen play out over and over again — the gap between how businesses define accountability and how software teams actually create value.

In most organizations, accountability for business teams is outcome-based — sales, customer growth, retention, pipeline health. But for software teams, it often becomes about schedule compliance: “Did we ship X by Y date?”

The problem is, software doesn’t work that way. It’s not a predictable assembly line; it’s a process of discovery and iteration. When we don’t define our own accountability, someone else will — and they’ll usually measure us by deadlines and feature lists rather than value creation.

A better approach is to think in terms of product bets — each with a desired outcome and an estimated value (the impact we believe it can create) and a maximum wager (how much we’re willing to invest to find out). It shifts the focus from rigid delivery to measuring, learning, and adjusting as we go.

It also pushes us to improve forecasting and understand value vs. non-value work — how much of our time truly moves the business forward versus what just keeps the lights on. That kind of transparency builds trust and makes engineering’s impact visible in business terms.

I’ve lived through the classic “when will you finish?” conversation many times. And it’s often because we haven’t clearly shown how our work connects to business value. The path to real accountability isn’t in perfect estimates — it’s in owning outcomes, making smarter bets, and demonstrating how software changes the business's trajectory.
